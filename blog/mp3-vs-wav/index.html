MP3 vs WAV: which audio format should you use?

A three-minute song can be about 30 MB as WAV or a few megabytes as MP3. Both can sound excellent; the right choice depends on whether you are still working on the audio or finished with it.

The core difference

WAV usually stores audio uncompressed: every sample the recorder captured is kept exactly. It is the standard working format in recording and editing software.

MP3 uses lossy compression. It removes sound information that human hearing is unlikely to notice, such as quiet sounds masked by louder ones at similar frequencies. The result is a file typically around a tenth of the WAV size, or smaller, that still sounds very close to the original at a good bitrate. Our explainer on lossy versus lossless compression covers the idea in general.

How much quality does MP3 lose?

It depends on the bitrate, the amount of data used per second of sound:

  • 128 kbps: acceptable for speech and casual listening; cymbals and reverb can sound slightly smeared.
  • 192 kbps: a good balance for music and voice. Most listeners cannot tell it from the original in normal conditions.
  • 256 to 320 kbps: very close to transparent for nearly everyone.

Which to use when

  • Recording and editing: WAV. Keep the original, edit, and export a compressed copy once at the end.
  • Sharing by email or chat, phone playback: MP3. Small and universally compatible.
  • Podcasts: edit in WAV, publish as MP3 (or AAC/M4A if your host prefers it).
  • Sending to a studio, video editor or broadcaster: follow their specification; often WAV.
  • Archiving important recordings: WAV or another lossless format such as FLAC, plus an MP3 copy for everyday listening.

Other formats you will meet

  • M4A/AAC: lossy like MP3, common on iPhones and in streaming; often slightly more efficient than MP3 at the same bitrate.
  • FLAC: lossless but compressed, roughly half the size of WAV with identical sound.
  • OGG/Opus: efficient lossy formats used in web apps and voice messages.

Quick rule

Work in WAV, share in MP3, and never convert back and forth repeatedly. Every MP3 encode is a small, permanent loss.
